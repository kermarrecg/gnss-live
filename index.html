How to read this

The big number tells you how unusual the atmosphere above the cluster is right now, compared with everything measured in the last two days. 50 means a completely ordinary moment. 90 means the air is in a state it reaches only a few times a day. Below 20, it is unusually quiet.

What we look for before convection: the patches of moist air grow bigger while the signal trembles more. Both panels rising together is the signature we documented on five networks and four continents. One panel alone means little.

What it is not. A high number does not mean it will rain here, and a low number does not mean it will not. This measures the state of the air, not the trigger. Storms need something to set them off, and that part happens at scales these antennas cannot see.

One station is not a signal. Individual receivers are noisy; only the cluster median (the thick blue line) carries information.

The three things we measure

Research experiment by Gael Kermarrec (Leibniz University Hannover). Signals come from low-cost receivers of the community-run Centipede network, processed in real time on the LUIS cluster with IGS orbit and clock corrections. We measure how the water-vapour delay fluctuates: the size of the moist patches and how much the signal trembles change as the boundary layer organises before convection. This is not a forecast and not a warning; official warnings are issued by Meteo-France.
