Eight small GPS antennas of the community-run Centipede network, all around Réunion Island, watch the water vapour above them every 5 minutes. Long before a storm, the moist and dry pockets of air stop being random and line up. The GPS signal, which crosses them on its way down, starts to tremble differently. This page shows how organised the air is right now, compared with the same time of day over the past 8 days.

The two observables behind the index

What is measured. Zenith wet delay (ZWD) at 30 s from PRIDE PPP (float ambiguities) with real-time CNES orbit and clock corrections (Galileo excluded: the receivers observe it on C1X/C5X, for which no consistent code biases exist). The ZWD fluctuation spectrum in sliding 62.5-minute windows is fitted with a Matérn model, giving a decorrelation rate λ (small λ = long-lived moisture structures) and an amplitude σ.

The index. Each parameter is turned into a causal anomaly z, its departure from the antenna's own values at the same time of day (±1 h) over the previous days (up to 8), in units of their interquartile range, never using the future. The index is z(1/λ) + z(σ): it rises when structures grow longer-lived and fluctuations grow stronger at the same time. The cluster value is the median across antennas, and the percentile places it among the same hours of the past 8 days.

What the signature means. On five radar-verified networks on four continents, the same joint rise precedes the first convective echo by hours. It reads the organisation of boundary-layer moisture, which the delay itself does not carry. Only the cluster median is interpreted, never a single antenna. This measures a state of the air, not a trigger, and is not a forecast.
